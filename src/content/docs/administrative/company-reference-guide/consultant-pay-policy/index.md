# Consultant Pay Policy

## Consultant Pay at Semantic Arts

This document describes the mechanics and the rationale for consultant pay at Semantic Arts.

### Nutshell

You get paid for all the hours you work. If you work 60 hours in each week, you will earn 50% more than if you had worked 40 hours that week.

Your hourly rate of pay will be your base pay rate plus a bonus rate.  The bonus rate may change slightly from pay period to pay period.  Currently the bonus is based primarily on your historical chargeability.  This document describes how we are currently determining this, but we reserve the right to modify it at any time.

Your hourly rate of pay will be based on how much revenue you generate from your own time spent on chargeable projects.  You will receive nearly half of the revenue you generate in pay, in some cases which will be described your earned revenue share may be slightly less than 50%.  It will be averaged over 52 weeks so your rate of pay will change gradually over time as you become more or less chargeable. If you have worked less than 52 weeks, it will be from the first pay period that you have enough earned revenue to qualify.

### Why We Are Not Salaried

There are two reasons we don’t pay annual salaries despite the fact that most firms do:

1. When you pay a consultant a salary all overtime is “free” to the employer which leads to high amounts of overtime and often high (often > 100%) chargeability.

1. Dubious legality: the regulations are fairly vague on qualifying to be exempt from overtime and to be able to pay salaries. We don’t want to be in a position where after the fact the regulations reclassify our employees.

### What You Can Expect

Rate of pay varies, but not much on a month-to-month basis as it is averaged over 52 weeks.  After an extended period of low or no chargeable work, your pay rate will decline.  We have been running this system for many years, and entry and staff consultants tend to make $80- $130K. Senior consultants tend to earn $140-230K with people working 5 to 10 percent overtime.

### Rationale

We have this pay system for several reasons:

- To share equitably – The value add in a professional services firm is generated in parts by the firm, its reputation, its methods, its ability to attract and retain customers, and partially by the actual consultants who perform the work.
- Focus – People pay attention to their pay, how it is derived, and what they can do to impact it.  In a traditional job (think HP), people have a job and they mostly try to be useful, but there isn't much connection between their pay and their contribution; there isn't much reminder on a day-to-day basis what it is that really makes a difference.
- Win/win – With this plan, we do not need to negotiate a pay package on a win/lose basis. With most pay plans, any concessions the employer gets out of the employee go directly to profit. Any concessions the employee gets out of the employer go into their pocket (and came out of profit).  With this plan, we want the employee to make more money.  When the employee makes more money so does the employer.
- To avoid layoffs – Most consulting companies have arranged their business model such that when average chargeability gets below about 70% they need to lay people off until they can return to profitability.  With our model, we can have extended periods of low chargeability and not need to invoke layoffs.
- To avoid burnout – The other side of the traditional consulting model is that it encourages high chargeability, which in turn tends toward long term out of town assignments.  These assignments burn people out. Most people cannot maintain a normal home and social life if they are spending more than 50% of their time out of town. Our model does not rely on long term out of town assignments; we generally tend to work on projects where the consultants can go onsite for a few days every several weeks and do most of the work back at the office.

### Why 50%

When we pay our consultants 50% of what they generated, we are actually paying closer to 60% of our revenue for two reasons:

1. There are additional costs borne by the firm directly proportional to the labor payments (the employer portions of social security, Medicare, IRA, % of healthcare benefits, as well as unemployment tax, worker’s compensation, and other costs that go up proportionately with payroll costs).
2. Mathematically, when someone transitions from highly chargeable to less chargeable, their percentage goes up.

The remaining 40% of our revenue goes into keeping the firm going. We allocate 20% to sales and marketing, which includes commissions, travel for trade shows and speaking engagements, and other marketing expenses; 10% of our revenue to overhead; 10% to contingency or profit.

### Detailed Calculations

There are three key numbers in the calculation:

·        Your total work hours for the last 52 weeks (the denominator).

·        Your total approved work hours for the current (4 week) pay period.

·        Your generated revenue for the last 52 weeks (the numerator).

### Work Hours

Work hours are hours you have worked on client - or Semantic Arts - initiated tasks.  Work that was assigned or that fits into our guidelines for pre-approved work will be considered “approved” time.  To date, all work for clients or Semantic Arts has been considered “approved,” but we reserve the right to withhold approval if someone is abusing the system.  The only distinction is that “unapproved” time is not eligible for the bonus (see **Bonus Rate** for more details).

There are categories in the timecard system listed as “unpaid” (e.g., exercise, errands, etc.), as opposed to Semantic Arts or a client.  This time is purely for record-keeping and to help you and the Company account for all your time.  You will not be paid for any of this personal time, and it will not factor into your pay rate.

### Generated Revenue

Your generated revenue is calculated as follows:

Your hours spent on each chargeable project

X             Your standard billing rate

X             (1 – the write-off % for a specific project)

=             Your generated revenue

Because in the first few months of your employment, small differences in chargeability or even timing of chargeability can have outsized effects on your pay, some of which can persist for a long period, we have made two adjustments to the algorithm.  The first is that the averaging period doesn't start until you have some significant billable time.  If you sign on, and then are in training or other non-billable time, we do not start the average until you become chargeable.

If your billing rate is $200 per hour and in the first year you have 1200 hours of chargeable work with an average write off of 10% you will have earned 200\*(1-10%) \*1200 or $216,000.  Half of that is $108,000.  If you also put in 1200 hours of non-chargeable time your average pay rate at the end of the year would be $45 an hour.  Your pay in the current period is $45 and hour, whether you are chargeable. If you are less chargeable than you were 12 months prior, your rate will average down at bit for the next pay period. If you work 200 hours in the current period, you will be paid $9,000 gross.

PTO, Sick Time and Holidays will be paid at the current rate, and those hours will not be added into the denominator.  If you took 4 weeks as vacation (160 hours) you would be paid $7,200 (no overtime on vacation), and the 160 will not be introduced into the denominator, reducing your pay rate.  (Your subsequent pay period rate still might go down depending on whether your previous year was front or rear loaded).

### Bonus Rate

Technically you are paid the base rate in the second column above, and the difference is a bonus. For purpose of pay stubs and w2s the two rates are combined into one. To be clear, the bonus amount can fluctuate up or down, although it typically does so very gradually due to the long averaging period.

### Retroactive Changes

If we get to the end of a fixed price (a.k.a., “not to exceed”) project, and there is an unexpected overrun, it will be retroactively applied to everyone who worked on that project because the overrun was likely not the cause of the team members who came in toward the end of the project to sort things out.

Unexpected write-offs are incurred in the pay period they were discovered. There are times we may choose to assign a project write-off from the beginning of the project, typically a marketing/sales decision to reduce our rates and will be clearly communicated.

### Over-runs and Under-runs are Not Symmetrical

If a project runs over, all the members of the team will participate in the write off.

If a fixed priced project comes in under the fixed price we negotiated, we do not write up the consultants’ generated revenue. The reason for this asymmetry is threefold:

1. The reason for the under run was likely not what the consultant did; it was either that we had performed a similar project previously and we were capitalizing on it, or we sold it well, or something similar.
2. Many of our projects have subjective deliverables.  If we said we were going to charge $100K for an ontology and the consultant knew they would get all of that if they finished early, they would be incented to turn in a trivial amount of work and attempt to claim full credit for it.  Our clients would realize that they received poor value and would be upset.
3. We'd rather have our consultants spending time on a project right up to the budget as long as they are increasing value to the client.  As a consultant goes over the budget, we'd still like them to deliver value; however, the consultant would note that their realized rate is going down and the value-to-input ratio is declining.

### What You can do to Increase Your Pay

Given all this you might be interested in what you can do to increase your pay. We've given this a great deal of thought.

The single most important thing you can do is be on chargeable projects.  Because the project managers (and the clients) have some discretion as to who gets on a project and how much time will be spent, the single most important thing you can do is do good work and collaborate on projects such that people would want you on their team.

While it’s best for your income to be on a project with a lower write-off, it’s better to be on a project with a higher write-off than to be on the bench. If you're not chargeable, you are not adding anything to the numerator while your work time is increasing the denominator, and therefore reducing your effective pay rate.  If you are working on a project with a high write-off, at least it is still adding to the numerator. Whether and how much your pay rate is declining (it could be increasing) depends on the pay period 13 months ago (weeks 56 through 53, which are just dropping off the average).

You might think raising your billing rate would increase you pay, and in general it will, but not as directly as you might think.  If you worked solo on a fixed priced project, say the 100-hour $20,000 project above and had no write off.  If your billing rate had been $300 / hour (instead of the $200 / hour) and the fixed price remained the project would end up with a 33% write off (your 100 hours at $300 per hour = $30K, which is $10K over the fixed fee.  $10K write off from the total $30K is 33%. And you would end up in the same spot.

Where a higher billing rate helps is on time and material projects, projects without a write off, or projects with other people on them (you will get a slightly larger share even if there is a write off).

We are setting billing rates primarily on market rates for skills and experience, coupled with what someone is able to do (someone who can manage a project at the same time they are participating in it are worth more to us that someone who can do one or the other).  Billing rates will also be adjusted for productivity and demand (if you are in high demand and therefore have higher utilization, we may consider bumping up your billing rate.  This may make you slightly less desirable to have on a project, but the increase billing rate should about balance that out.)
