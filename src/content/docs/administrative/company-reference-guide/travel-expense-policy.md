## Background

Most clients dislike the idea of paying for nonproductive time.  As an example, I worked at a client that had another consulting firm come in, who had a policy of consultants traveling during the day and charging all their travel time to the client. Somehow, they managed to staff this west coast project with nothing but east coast consultants.  As a result, the client ended up paying for five days of consulting and getting three.  While this was profitable for the consulting company and a breeze for the consultants, that epitomizes taking advantage of your client.  In the long term, this sort of behavior catches up with you.

At the other extreme are the more typical consulting firms that expect the consultants to be on-site full time and travel after hours.  This means months of traveling Sunday afternoons and getting home late Friday.  This is a prescription for burnout.  Also, these consulting firms tend to be salaried, so the extra time does not cost the employer anything.

## Our Policy

First, we try to keep the travel requirements to a manageable amount.  There is a great deal of value, not just in the delivery of our services but also in bonding and finding new opportunities, to provide some of our services on site. Spending the majority of our time on-site though is counterproductive: it takes on the flavor of staff augmentation or even quasi-employment, which leads to becoming one of them (making it harder to be a change agent, and harder to stay out of the day-to-day meetings that don’t concern us).

Hence our strong preference for the three days every three-week cadence.  This also has the added benefit of almost always being able to be done at 10% of our fees (for out-of-pocket expenses).  Most clients don’t balk at 10% when they actually get something for it.  In the example, in the opening paragraph, the client was paying close to 100% more than they were getting, between paying for two days of consulting fees for every three days spent, plus the out of pocket, which because it was incurred every week, bringing the total to more than double the actual value.

The main exception to this is overseas travel.  We have done a few overseas trips and will likely do a few more.  For extremely long trips (Abu Dhabi or the far east, which can be 20+ hours) we will attempt to expressly negotiate something reasonable into our contract.  Whatever we negotiate will override these general guidelines in those cases.

In general, we encourage our consultants to use as much of their travel time as possible, productively.  The ideal is if you have work for the client you’re traveling to, that you can do in route.  Barring that, we recommend storing up work that you can do in route, perhaps that is preparing presentations, writing white papers, designing internal ontologies, working on skill-building, or reading (not talking about novels here).

Our experience is no matter how well you plan you cannot use all of your transit time productively (waiting in line, boarding, etc).  You are permitted to charge **two hours each way** for travel within North America and **four hours each way** for other international travel to the task called “Client Satisfaction - Travel to Client.”  In the event that your trip requires an unscheduled overnight stay due to flight cancellations or the like, you may charge **an additional two****hours** for the second day. This is a non-billable task.  You will get paid for your time, but it won’t add to your earned revenue.  Any other time in transit, if not working, is unpaid. *For travel for conferences or other non-client but work travel, you can use the task “Travel To/From Conference” under the Skills Upgrade task in Spark.*

We see this time as far less than a traditional commute (a half-hour one-way commute is 15 hours every three weeks) and hope you can endure it for the good of the client.

If you happen to be someone who can’t or doesn’t want to work in transit (and prefers to sleep, watch movies, or read novels) feel free to do so.  This is your personal choice, but don’t charge that time to either the client or the firm.

In terms of arrival, try to arrive early enough that you aren’t sleep-deprived when you come to the client.  I generally try to arrive in time for dinner (maybe a late dinner) which I find is a good time to catch up with the team (and have a nice dinner on the firm).

In general, try to get most of your last day in at the client.  Leaving their office 4:00 ish is generally not a problem but ducking out a half-day early eventually starts to rub clients the wrong way.

You may charge the time it takes to plan the trip to “Travel Admin Logistics” (also not billable).  If you find this is taking a lot of time, we may have a travel planning training event (Often, it is possible to fit the logistics in around other tasks – it’s the perfect thing to do if a meeting ends ten minutes early).

## Expenses

There are generally two types of expenses that are reimbursable - overhead expenses charged to the firm, and expenses related to client travel. We will reimburse legitimate expenses incurred in the service of our clients including meals, travel, and other incidentals (printing, shipping, etc.).  Overhead expenses that will be reimbursed might include conference or event travel, educational materials, etc. We would like you to treat our money and our client’s money as if it were your own.  What we mean by that is to not be extravagant just because it isn’t coming out of your pocket; but at the same time, don’t be so frugal that you create problems.  For example, don’t route yourself through an extra city on the way to a client to save $100.  (You might consider it to save $1,000, but there are probably other ways to skin that cat.)

Here are some additional rules to follow when submitting expenses:

- Do not submit expenses for multiple trips on one report and try to keep the number of reports per trip to a minimum.

  - In general, a trip should have two reports - one for expenses incurred before travel (airfare for instance) and one for all expenses incurred while traveling. There are times expenses might come in late and require a new report, but it should be an exception.
  - **Do not put multiple trips on a single report.** You should have a “clean” report for each time you travel and submit them separately, even if sent on the same day
- All expenses **must** include a photo of the original receipt. There may be times this is not possible, in which case you can share a screenshot of the charge on your credit card statement or other proof of the expense. If your purchase covered more than just you, note that as well (this might be the case where you bought dinner for the team on your card).
- All individual expenses must be tagged in Expensify with the appropriate tag. If you don’t see one that is relevant, contact the office and request one be created. Untagged expenses will be flagged and not paid until remedied
- For each expense, you must document a description when not totally clear. For example, include which meal it was and the date (Dinner - 5/12/25). For flights and hotels, include in the description your dates of travel (Hotel for night of 5/12 through 5/14).
- Expenses must be submitted promptly to be reimbursed.  Expenses are due 14 days after your return from the trip. Expenses beyond 14 days may not be reimbursed. If you have a special case why delayed submission is needed, please notify the office.
- Expenses must be submitted by end of day Tuesday before the end of the pay period. Expenses submitted after this will result in payment delayed to the following period.
- In Expensify, please do not retract reports. Once it has been approved, if there are any issues please email [office@semanticarts.com](mailto:office@semanticarts.com) for resolution.

If you are unsure about anything, have questions or just want to communicate clearly the circumstances, please don’t hesitate to reach out to the office. It’s impossible to cover every possible scenario with a policy, so at times we can make decisions that “make sense” for a given scenario.

Failure to follow these guidelines or to respond promptly to requests for modifications or information may result in delayed payment. Keep in mind that with 4-week payrolls, if we have to delay reimbursement due to failure to follow the policy or respond to questions, you are not going to be reimbursed for nearly another month.

## Travel Booking Guidelines

Employees are responsible for booking their own travel and accommodations. Below are some general guidelines that should apply in most situations. If you are unsure or have questions, feel free to reach out to the project manager, office manager or Dave for further assistance. Do not purchase trip insurance, as the restrictions on what it can be used for are extremely limited.

### Transportation Guidelines

- Generally, an uber/lyft is preferable to a rental car, though not in all circumstance. Stick with rental car companies that you’ve dealt with before that are near the airport.  Get only as much car as you need (compact or intermediate for most trips) and waive all the insurance. If you can’t get a reasonable deal, hotwire car rental is almost always a good deal, just make sure you select “at the airport” and not a remote location. You must have your own insurance that will cover liability and collision on your rental car or a credit card with this provision.
- The question of refundable versus non-refundable fares requires some judgment.  Our clients do sometimes change travel dates on us after we have booked our airfare.  In general, they will pay for the change fees we incur, but we still want to keep them to a minimum.  However fully refundable airfares are often twice as expensive as nonrefundable, so unless you have an expectation greater than 50% that a client will change the trip, the premium is not worth it. You will be reimbursed for the change fees, but let's keep them manageable.  If you think that a client may change their on-site dates, and if Southwest is an option, they are generally the way to go, as their reimbursement policy is so much better than other sites. In general booking more than a few weeks in advance rarely reduces the fare and introduces more time for things to change.
- Choice of airline or hotel should be based on appropriate economy and convenience, and not on your frequent flyer of choice.  You should not book a flight if an equivalent one is available for more than 10% less.  Get a frequent flyer account with all airlines and hotel chains and this will be less of an issue.
- Sometimes there might be an option to take a train or drive instead of fly. You are welcome to select your preference here, but we will only reimburse (and charge the client) for the cheapest option. If you choose an alternative to flying, find at least two flights you could have taken and note the total costs in your expense report
- If you drive your personal vehicle to/from the airport, you are eligible to have your mileage reimbursed per IRS guidelines and at the established IRS rate. Please note this is not available if you travel in any vehicle that is not your own. Shuttles or ubers to/from the airport are also reimbursable.
- If you are adding personal travel on to your firm travel, we can usually make this work but need to be in communication. For instance, if you depart a city a few days late in favor of some personal time, you should document what the airfare would have been had you left at the regular time. If your actual airfare is drastically different the firm would only cover the amount it would have cost to leave at the end of the scheduled trip.
- We will pay (and try to pass on to our client) up to $50 per segment for "economy plus" seating or its equivalent, if you commit to using the extra space to work on the flight.
- The question as to how far in advance to book is interesting. It used to be the shorter the time between booking and travelling the more expensive the fare. It is often true for same day (but even then, it’s surprising how often a last-minute ticket is reasonable). Try this yourself: go on kayak or your booking site of choice and put in a destination for a few days from now and a few months from now. Generally, it’s good to book a few weeks in advance, but longer than that is flirting with plan changes.
- Kayak seems to be a pretty good site that allows what they call “hacker fees” sometimes flying a different airline out and back or even flying in and out of a different airport in the same city.
- If you think Southwest is an option on your route, open another tab and go direct to Southwest to check.
- Many cities have more than one airport. A further away airport might be worth it if it is either much cheaper or has better timing.
- We generally try to fly non-stop if we can (fewer things to go wrong) but its occasionally worth it to consider. The way I do it without having to look at hundreds of flights is to select the slider that kayak calls “Flight leg” (which really is total trip duration) and crank it down to something within reason, this eliminates a lot of the infeasible solutions. Every once in a while, a connecting flight will pop up that is more attractive than the non-stops.
- Occasionally decomposing the trip into two one-way trips finds options that didn’t show up otherwise (logically this shouldn’t be true, but it seems to be – especially if one leg is Southwest and the other someone else).
- Feel free to ignore Frontier and Spirit. By the time you add in all their ala carte charges (carry on bag, assigned seat, seat with a tray table etc. etc. you’ve added $120-$150 to the total) you’re back up to the airfares of their competitors.
- You keep your own frequent flyer miles. In some cases, if airfare has been exceedingly high and it’s possible to use frequent flyer miles, we will ask to buy them from you. The current going rate is the lesser of 2 cents per mile or the lowest reasonable fare available. Let’s say the normal RT to NYC is $450 but this trip they have spiked to where $750 is the best you can get. First document with screen captures that there weren’t any better reasonable fares available.  If you can get an RT for 20,000 miles, we will pay you the lesser of $750 or 2 cents per mile ($400), therefore $400.  If it takes 40,000 miles to get the RT then the reimbursement would be the lesser of $750 and $800, or $750.  In any event, you are not obligated to use your miles for this purpose, if $750 if the best available fare and we can document this, then that is what we will charge.
- Occasionally, there is an opportunity to stay over somewhere rather than fly home.  Maybe you’re at a conference and the client work starts the following Monday.  If you opt to stay in a hotel somewhere rather than flying home, enter the hotel expense with the note “in lieu of flight” assuming the hotel is less than the round-trip flight.  We will book it as if it were a flight, but the supporting documentation will make it clear what we did (and that we saved our clients’ money doing this).

### Lodging Guidelines

- Avoid prepaying for hotels through the web; they are impossible to change at the last minute and we end up absorbing that expense.
- Stay at conservative hotels (three stars, typically).
- Don’t put any expenses on your room.  If you get a meal, drink, etc., put it on your credit card.  We want to make the billing and reimbursement as simple as possible.  We don’t bill for actual meals, so we have a lot of extra accounting and explaining to do when there are other charges on the room.
- You should get “hotel tonight” on your cell phone in case you need a last-minute hotel. They often have deals that we haven’t seen elsewhere but primarily when you are stuck with getting a hotel really at the last minute.
- Watch for hidden fees. Hotels are shifting more of their price into something often called a “resort fee.” This makes some hotels look cheaper on a per night basis, but when you add in the “resort fee” (which isn’t optional) and the taxes it can add a great deal to the total. Airbnb are doing a lot of the same with cleaning and various other fees. Compare on the total cost, not just the cost per night.
- On refundability: usually you can get a good deal with hotels that are either completely refundable or refundable if you cancel a day or two in advance. Even the non-refundable ones only commit you to one night. It is rare (but happens occasionally) that a hotel (or more often an Airbnb) with a strict no cancellation/no refund policy is so good that it’s worth taking the risk on, but mostly it isn’t necessary.

### Meal Guidelines

- We almost always charge clients per diem for meals, and we will pay you for actual.  Be reasonable, however; if you’re going to splurge on anything, a nice dinner on the road would be the item.  Don’t brag about going to fancy restaurants; clients will think they are paying us too much.
- If you purchase a meal for more than just yourself, make sure to indicate that on your expense report.
